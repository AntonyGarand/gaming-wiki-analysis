The experiment01

A very silly game, taken seriously

Start at Falador, the white city. Its first link is Capital, because Falador is one. Capital's first link is Kingdom. Kingdom's is Gielinor, the world the game runs on. Gielinor's is Guthix, the god who shaped it. Guthix's is Gods. And Gods points back at Gielinor. Five clicks from a castle to a circle. You can keep clicking forever; the circle doesn't care.

People noticed years ago that on Wikipedia this game always ends the same way. About 97% of articles drain into one loop, and the loop runs through Philosophy. The game caught on because it feels like it should mean something: click enough first links and you find out what an encyclopedia thinks everything is made of. Wikipedia's answer is philosophy, which is very Wikipedia of it. We wanted the gaming version of that answer.

To get it, we fetched fifteen wikis — this one, RuneScape 3 and RuneScape Classic, Minecraft, Terraria, GTA, Fortnite, Factorio, Tarkov, and seven more — and followed every first link of every article. Each bar shows how many of that wiki's articles end up in a loop before their walk dies. GTA, a guide to a fictional America, is the best-behaved of the big wikis at 87%. OSRS is right behind at —. Factorio barely participates: 44%, and more than half its walks just die. Hover a bar to see what that wiki believes in.

The rules02

The rules, such as they are

A wiki offers a hundred ways to cheat at this game. Infoboxes and nav templates are packed with links, but nobody reads those as sentences, so the walk ignores them. Links in parentheses are disambiguators, like Dragon (race); italics usually mark titles and foreign words. Skipped, skipped. Links that land on disambiguation pages — the "X may refer to…" junctions, 1,568 of them here — and on date pages like 28 April, which are update logs wearing article costumes, are skipped too. Red links, images, categories: skipped. If the first link fails a test, the walk takes the next one. If a page offers nothing that passes, the walk ends there and the article goes in the graveyard column.

What survives all that skipping is the part of the wiki that explains itself in sentences: the chain of read-this-first that a real reader falls down. The panel is running one right now, on a random OSRS article. Every pill is one click. They come out shorter than you'd think.

Convergence03

Half-life of a click

On this wiki, — of — articles fall into a loop before they run out of links. Count the hops each one takes first, and you get a bell curve, as tidy as anything in an economics textbook. The median article joins its loop in — clicks; the longest walk in the entire wiki is 18. Editors keep definitions short on purpose. A reference work that recursed forever would be unusable, and everyone involved knows it.

Turn the histogram into a running total and the funnel shows up. Nearly everything that ever converges has converged by hop ten; the tail after that is the long-winded minority, articles hiking through twenty links of lore before they find their whirlpool. The dashed line is the median, so half the wiki is home by then. One comparison surprised us: OSRS and GTA tie for the deepest median walk of the fifteen wikis, six hops, while RuneScape 3 and Terraria wrap up in three. This wiki defines its subjects in terms of deeper subjects more often than almost anyone else. Its readers read the lore. All of it.

The attractors04

Three gods and a monster manual

A loop that articles drain into is an attractor, and the articles draining into it are its basin. Run the strict walk over the whole wiki and one loop takes a third of everything: Gielinor → Guthix → Gods. The world, the god who made the world, and gods in general. Strip off the XP grinds and the margin trading, and this encyclopedia has decided that the most fundamental thing about its game is that it's a place with a theology. In second place, Monster → Combat → Bestiary at 28%: the monster manual, the other thing this game is about.

Third place goes to Currencies → Coins, at 15%: the economy is this game's third religion, which tracks — for a lot of players the Grand Exchange is the endgame and everything else is a delivery mechanism for it. Between the world, the monsters and the money, roughly three quarters of the wiki has a god to fall into. The remainder splits among dozens of much smaller congregations, and that is where it gets good.

Below the big basins it gets weird. Vampyre → Vampyrium. Grain → Wheat. Shields → Square shield. Zanaris → Fairy (race). Each of these loops holds a few dozen faithful articles, whirlpools the main current never notices. The chart grows from the top three basins to the top nine; the tail continues long past where the chart stops.

The census05

What the neighbours worship

Line the fifteen up and each one's answer is on display. Terraria's is itemisation itself: Items → World, 67% of the wiki. Fortnite's ends at Epic Games → Tim Sweeney, 84% of it, a corporation and then a person, in that order. Minecraft bottoms out at Block → Slab, two shapes. GTA drains into Scotland → United Kingdom, an actual country, which is on-brand for a series that keeps insisting it's satire. And the RuneScape wikis are a family portrait: OSRS believes in the world, Gielinor → Guthix → Gods. RuneScape 3 believes in the pantheon, Elder Gods → Gods → Elder artefacts. RuneScape Classic, preserved exactly as the game died, believes in the two men who made it: Andrew Gower → Jagex, 44%.

The table ranks the wikis by how much of themselves drains into their single top loop. Balatro, a poker roguelite with 517 pages, is the most devout wiki we measured: 81.8% of its articles end in Jokers → Tarot cards → Consumables. Factorio is the mirror image. Its top loop holds 7.8%, and more than half its walks die without finding any loop at all, a wiki of engineers who will not be told what anything is. OSRS lands in the middle: enough consensus to keep a god, enough sects to keep the god busy.

The decay of consensus06

By the fifth link, everyone disagrees

Everything so far used the first link, the sentence editors fight over most. There's no law saying the game stops there. So we re-ran all 36,164 walks five more times: second link, third, fifth, last, and one run where every click is random.

Convergence drops from 85% to 29% by the fifth link. The top attractor drops faster, from 31% of the wiki to 9%. The trinity, it turns out, is an editing habit, not a law of nature. Editors agree sharply on what earns the first mention and agree on almost nothing by the fifth. Factorio, for scale, starts where OSRS ends: 44% on its first link.

The dotted line shows where the missing agreement went: into dead ends. At the higher ranks most walks simply run out of links, and the network comes apart. Follow last links and 56% of articles still find loops — oddly, better than the fifth link's 29% — though the wiki shatters into 814 separate little worlds doing it. The random rank lands at 45%: pick links with no plan at all, and nearly half of all articles still find a loop. That's one roll of the dice per article, seeded by page id, so read it as about 45%. The structure lives in the graph itself, and you can't click your way out of a graph.

Hubs & bridges07

Famous pages, load-bearing pages

A wiki has two kinds of importance, and they barely overlap. The visible kind is degree: the pages that the most other pages link to. On OSRS the list is topped by Music, with 855 inbound links, then interface items, jingles, and clue guides. No gods, no dragons, no bosses. The most-linked pages in any wiki turn out to be the plumbing: the shared vocabulary every article leans on without thinking about it.

The quieter kind is betweenness: pages that sit between other pages, where deleting one would snap the wiki's explanatory paths. This is where the lore finally shows up. OSRS's load-bearing pages are Player character, then Human, then List of races. Nearly every monster and NPC definition passes through the question of what counts as you, which is a lot of philosophy per drop table.

The constellation08

The map that looks like outer space

Draw the OSRS network as a night sky and it cooperates. The loops are stars, tributaries stream toward them, dead-end chains drift at the edge like comets nobody claimed. Node size is inbound links, and every arrow follows one article's first link, so the picture really is the direction the wiki thinks in. Hover a node to trace its path to the loop it belongs to. Click one and you're on the live wiki, reading the real article.

Your turn09

Feed it an article

Type anything and watch it walk: a boss, a quest, an item, a city. Guess which whirlpool it falls into before the pills stop. You get scarily good at this. Then switch the rank and follow the same article's fifth link instead. It usually ends somewhere completely different, occasionally somewhere better.

Caveats, for honesty's sake. Everything here is one snapshot of wikis that change every day. The parser trips over the occasional exotic template, and when it does, that page's walk is wrong — we believe this is rare, and we would say that either way. And the walk's rules — skip the parentheses, skip the italics — are a house style rather than a law of nature; every link at every rank is counted the same way.
