Part 1 · The idea

Click the first link. Then keep going.

Start at Falador, the white city. The first link on its page is Capital, because that's what Falador is. Capital's first link is Kingdom. Kingdom's is Gielinor, the world the game takes place in. Gielinor's is Guthix, the god who made it. Guthix's is Gods, and Gods links straight back to Gielinor. Five clicks from a castle to a loop, and once you're in the loop you can keep clicking forever.

That's the whole game. Wikipedia readers found it years ago and called it Getting to Philosophy, because on Wikipedia nearly every walk ends in the same loop, the one that passes through Philosophy. It caught on because it feels like it means something: follow enough "X is a…" sentences and you find out what the encyclopedia thinks everything boils down to.

So we tried it on game wikis. We downloaded of them, articles in total, and followed the first link of every single page. This article mostly follows one wiki, the Old School RuneScape Wiki, and uses the other for comparison. Every number in the text is read straight from the data, so if one looks odd, at least it's honestly odd.

Why game wikis? Wikipedia covers everything, so its big loop is bound to be abstract. A game wiki covers one small world, written by people who all played the same game. When their first links agree, they agree about something specific.
Part 2 · The rules

What counts as the first link?

A wiki page has plenty of links that would spoil the game, so we follow a few rules. Links in infoboxes and navigation boxes don't count, because nobody reads those as sentences. Anything in parentheses is skipped, since it's nearly always a note like Dragon (race). Anything in italics is skipped too, since it's usually a title or a foreign word. We also skip links to disambiguation pages and to date pages, which on a game wiki are really just update logs. Red links, images, files and categories are all skipped.

The walker takes the first link that passes every rule. If nothing on the page passes, the walk ends there and the article goes in the dead-end pile. What's left is the part of the wiki that explains things in sentences, the chain a real reader would actually follow.

The panel is running one of these walks on a random article. Each row is one click, and the red rows at the bottom are the loop. The bar beside each page shows how many other articles' walks pass through it, so you can watch a lonely walk merge into busier and busier traffic until it hits the loop. Part 7 · Busy pages

The pages everyone passes through

Every map has a few places everyone passes through. On a wiki there are two kinds, and they barely overlap. The obvious kind is the busy page: the one that the most other articles pick as their first link. Here the busiest is , picked first by articles, then and .

No gods, no dragons. The most-picked pages are basic vocabulary, the shared words every article leans on without thinking about it. That's exactly why they aren't attractors. They're places you pass through, not places you stay.

The quieter kind is the bridge: a page in the middle of other articles' walks, where removing it would break thousands of chains at once. This is where the lore shows up. Part 10 · Your turn

Try it yourself

Now it's your turn. Type any article and watch it walk: a boss, a quest, an item, a city. Guess which loop it will land in before the pills stop. You'll get surprisingly good at this after a few tries. Then switch the rank and follow the same article's fifth link instead. It usually ends somewhere completely different.

The Charts button at the top of the page points every chart, the map and the walkers at a different wiki. The text stays on Old School RuneScape, but the pictures will follow you anywhere.

Caveats. Everything here is one snapshot of wikis that change daily. The rules above are a house style, not a law of nature; change "skip italics" to "keep italics" and some basins will shift. The parser trips over the occasional odd template, and when it does, that one page's walk is wrong. The random rank is a single seeded draw per article, so read its numbers as roughly right rather than exact.
